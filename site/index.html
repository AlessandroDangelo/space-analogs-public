Challenge

Why look for places on Earth that resemble the Moon and Mars.

What NASA asked

Use open data to find and describe places on Earth that resemble future bases on the Moon and landing sites on Mars, and show them on a map or interactive tool.

Read the full challenge

Challenge “Identify Earth Locations that Analog the Permanent Moon Base Locations and Mars”.

Choosing where to land and where to build a base on the Moon or Mars depends on many factors: terrain, geology, environment and available resources. Places on Earth with similar features, such as the Atacama Desert, Haughton crater and Death Valley, let teams test equipment and methods before flight, but they are still poorly characterized.

The challenge is to use open data from Earth, the Moon and Mars to identify and characterize terrestrial analogs for future bases and landing sites (deserts, polar regions, volcanic and arid terrain) and present the result as a map, a visualization or another tool.

Paraphrase of the official NASA Space Apps Challenge 2026 statement.

Why identify analog environments on Earth?

  • Technology validationTest equipment and solutions under conditions similar to those on the Moon and Mars.
  • Lower riskFind failures and limitations before sending technology on space missions.
  • Lower costAvoid unnecessary experimental missions and investment in unvalidated equipment.
  • Space sustainabilityReduce the waste left by failed missions and abandoned equipment.
  • Environmental protectionMinimize the impacts of space exploration and the risk of contaminating other worlds.
  • Readiness for future missionsDevelop more reliable technology for exploring and operating beyond Earth.

Our answer: for each target on the Moon and Mars, a ranking of the most similar places on Earth, and how they match (terrain, ground cover, temperature or geology).

    Methodology

    How we turned maps of three worlds into one comparable ranking, in 7 steps.

    1. 1 Tiles

      Each place becomes up to 9 tiles of 9×9 km.

      Details

      Each place becomes up to 9 tiles of 9×9 km on a 40 m grid, the finest grid shared by the data of all three bodies. That way a place does not depend on a single point.

    2. 2 Terrain

      Slope, roughness and relief of each tile.

      Sources and resolution

      Elevation models: NASADEM and ArcticDEM/REMA on Earth, LOLA (20 m) on the Moon, CTX (20 m) on Mars. We compute slope, detrended roughness (small-scale irregularities only) and relief.

    3. 3 Ground cover

      How much of the ground is bare rock, ice, vegetation or city.

      Sources and resolution

      ESA WorldCover (10 m) on Earth and the AADC rock outcrop map for Antarctica. On the Moon and Mars the whole surface is bare.

    4. 4 Temperature

      Mean and maximum annual surface temperature.

      Sources and resolution

      NASA POWER (MERRA-2) on Earth, Diviner (LRO) on the Moon and the NASA Ames climate model on Mars: annual mean and maximum.

    5. 5 Geology

      Rock type in 6 classes shared by the three bodies.

      Sources and resolution

      Geologic maps (Macrostrat, USGS Moon 1:5M, USGS Mars 1:20M) translated into 6 shared classes: volcanic, crystalline, sedimentary, evaporite, ice and impact.

    6. 6 Similarity

      A 0–100 score, with equal weight for the 4 groups.

      Details

      We compare each tile of the target with the most similar tile of each place on Earth, with equal weight for terrain, ground cover, temperature and geology, and show which group makes them alike.

    7. 7 AI and validation

      Second and third opinions, and tests against published lists.

      Details

      ZEUS clusters the tiles as a second opinion, and a Mars climate layer (Luís G. O. Miranda: NASA Ames model × 1 year of Meteomatics data) gives a third. We validate against ESA’s Catalogue of Planetary Analogues and against control places (cities and rainforest).

    Why these features

    • Slope: landing safety, effort for rovers and astronauts.
    • Roughness: boulders and grooves that stall wheels and landing legs.
    • Relief: line-of-sight communication, shadow and lighting.
    • Temperature: thermal design of equipment, batteries and suits.
    • Ground cover: dust, exposed surface and ice as a resource.
    • Geology: field science, sampling and use of local resources.

    Validation

    AUC measures whether the method ranks the right places above the others: 0.5 = chance; 1 = perfect separation.
